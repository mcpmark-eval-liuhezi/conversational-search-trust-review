## Summary

This paper asks how much people trust health information that comes out of LLM-powered conversational search, and how the interface that disseminates it changes that trust, framing the work with the MAIN model of trust in technology. It reports two mixed-methods studies, each pairing a laboratory session with semi-structured interviews. In Study 1 (N=21), a within-subjects design, participants completed six health search tasks spanning three question types — general health, symptom/cause, and treatment — using Google and ChatGPT (GPT-4, no web browsing), with agent and task order counterbalanced. Trust in health information was significantly higher for ChatGPT than for Google (mean 4.05 vs. 3.77 on a 5-point scale; main effect of agent F(1,20)=6.73, p=.017), and — importantly — the type of health question had no significant effect on trust, so the agent gap held across all three task types. Notably, trust in the information correlated strongly with trust in the agent for Google (r=.63, p=.003) but not for ChatGPT, suggesting users judge synthesized LLM answers on their intrinsic presentation (logical flow, professional language, tailoring) rather than on the agent's reputation. Study 2 (N=20) then isolated the dissemination interface by comparing a text-based chatbot, a speech-based Amazon Echo Dot, and a custom embodied agent with a physical body and smartphone face — all powered by the very same GPT-4o backend. Trust again varied significantly across interfaces, with text rated most trustworthy (4.19 vs. 4.13 speech and 4.00 embodied) and a mediation analysis showing usability fully mediated the interface-to-trust relationship (indirect effect β=.154, p<.001; direct effect non-significant). Interviews across both studies identified the trust drivers: source credibility, users' search autonomy moderated by prior health knowledge, familiarity, usability, interaction style, and modality — with voice and embodiment adding cognitive load, privacy concerns, authenticity worries, and low tolerance for small glitches such as avatar expression lag. The authors conclude that conversational search genuinely outperforms traditional search on perceived trustworthiness for health, but that more human-like modality is not a shortcut to trust: what matters is a credible agent delivered through a usable, transparent, familiar interface.

## Direct takeaways

- **ChatGPT > Google on trust.** Participants trusted health information from ChatGPT significantly more than from Google (M=4.05 vs. 3.77; F(1,20)=6.73, p=.017), and also rated the agent itself as more trustworthy (t(21)=-2.53, p=.02, d=0.55).
- **The gap is task-robust.** Question type (general / symptom-and-cause / treatment) had no significant main effect on trust and did not interact with agent, so the preference for conversational search is generalized rather than task-specific.
- **Trust decoupling for LLMs.** For Google, trust in information tracked trust in the agent; for ChatGPT it did not. With no explicit source to check, users fell back on presentation heuristics (logical flow, professional wording, personalization) as a proxy for credibility.
- **Text beat speech and embodiment — same LLM, different trust.** With the identical GPT-4o backend, the text-based CUI was trusted most (4.19 vs. 4.13 speech, 4.00 embodied), refuting the assumption that modality richness linearly increases trust.
- **Usability is the mediator.** Usability fully mediated the interface-to-trust-in-information relationship, making ease of use a prerequisite for trust rather than a nice-to-have.
- **Modality has a downside.** Speech and embodied interfaces raised privacy concerns (e.g., speaking health queries aloud), authenticity doubts, and a much lower tolerance for small technical errors, which immediately "broke the illusion of competence."
- **Search autonomy and familiarity matter.** Trust was shaped by how much control users felt over their own seeking process (moderated by prior knowledge) and by how closely an interface matched their habitual health-search behaviors.
- **Design guidance.** Prioritize usable, transparent, familiar text-forward dissemination; make personalization legible with visible cues about when personal data informed a response; and move beyond binary accept/reject consent toward granular, context-sensitive privacy controls for health data.
